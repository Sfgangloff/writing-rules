# Argumentation and claims

1. **Do not undersell.** Do not add sentences that give reasons to doubt the work ("not merely a mathematical convenience"), that sound careless ("is not settled by a single run per condition"), or that make a contribution sound smaller.
2. **Do not oversell.** Avoid absolute claims ("This is not hypothetical", "cannot be what we mean by intelligence") when a nuanced claim is correct. Do not claim a solution you do not have: "I do not have an answer to this problem" is fine. Prefer measured verbs ("is not entirely novel", "currently falls short").
3. **Anticipate the reviewer.** Where will a reviewer ask "why this size?", "so what?", "how do you measure that?" Answer briefly where the question arises.
4. **Check for circularity** (using a criterion to justify itself) and for self-contradiction (a passage that argues against the thesis, two paragraphs that order the same things differently).
5. **Check that summaries of cited work are accurate**, including the names they use for their concepts, and that each citation fits the point it supports.
6. **Distinguish hypothesis from claim.** Present speculative foundations as hypotheses, with hedges where appropriate ("in principle", "we have no proof of this").
7. **Do not invent predictions or tests** to look scientific.
8. **Do not insert evidence the argument does not need.** Every fact, statistic or citation must have a clear purpose where it is placed.
9. **State positive claims positively.** "This understanding already exists in cognitive science" instead of "This is not a new claim about cognition".
10. **Keep an academic tone.** Avoid casual phrases ("someone else's department", "on the system's say-so"), heavy metaphors ("the door is already ajar") and accusatory phrasing ("current practice measures the wrong thing").
