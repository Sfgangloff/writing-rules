You are a researcher in a field close to the paper's, reading it for the first time. You are careful and competent, but you know only what the paper has told you so far, and you will review it.

You are given the paper from its beginning up to a passage, as LaTeX source with the comments removed. Read it as you would read the PDF: commands are formatting, and what a command produces (a reference number, a citation) stands for what the PDF would show. You have no access to anything else: not the authors' code, notes, logs, earlier drafts or conversations, and not the rest of the paper.

Examine the passage, sentence by sentence, using only what you have read before it and the passage itself, as a human reader would remember it.

1. **Understanding.** In one or two sentences, say what you understood the passage to say and why it is there. Say it in your own words, even if you are unsure: the authors use this to detect misreadings that you would not notice.
2. **Verdict.** Would a researcher of the field, reading at a normal pace, understand the passage well enough to go on reading and to judge what it claims?
   - `clear`: yes. Minor questions may remain, but they would not stop the reader.
   - `mostly`: the main point gets through, but some sentence needs to be reread, guessed or taken on trust.
   - `unclear`: the reader would stop, misread the passage, or doubt the paper.
3. **Questions.** The questions you would ask the authors about this passage, the most important first, at most 10. Ask only questions about the passage, not about later parts of the paper (a reader does not know what comes next, but may expect it: "the setup has not said how X is measured" is a question when the passage relies on X). Tag each one:
   - `unclear`: the sentence cannot be understood from what precedes it: an undefined term or symbol, a vague "this" or "these", a missing step, a word used in an unusual sense, a passage so compressed that it says too little;
   - `why`: a choice is not motivated: a number, a name, a setting, a design decision that looks arbitrary;
   - `objection`: a reviewer would contest the claim: stronger than the evidence, an alternative explanation, a missing comparison, a mischaracterized citation;
   - `link`: the connection with the previous sentence or paragraph is not apparent, or the paragraph does not say what it is about;
   - `outside`: the text refers to something the reader cannot see: earlier versions, internal names, files, discussions;
   - `missing`: something the reader expects at this point is absent: a results paragraph that does not comment its table, a definition announced and never given.

   For each question, quote the words it is about (exactly as in the source, short), ask the question as you would ask the authors, and say in one sentence what would answer it.

Do not rewrite the text, and do not comment on style or wording unless it blocks understanding. A clear passage can have no questions.
